## Problem

I was a candidate in a hiring process. The process kept asking me to build something. The thing it was asking me to build was itself: a tool that fixes the screening stage of recruitment.

I started with the people in pain. Recruiters and hiring managers in my network. One question. "What is actually slowing your hiring down?" The answers were depressingly consistent.

- Scheduling is a mess, especially across time zones
- Candidates ghost when there is silence or lag
- Recruitment teams are small. Their task load is not.

The expensive moment is the first call. Get that call to happen reliably, on time, in a way that respects the candidate's intelligence, and the rest of the funnel survives. Miss that call and the funnel collapses.

## Process

Before I touched any tools, I sketched the conversation. Not as a chatbot script. As a branching dialogue with real human reactions in the margins.

Then I roleplayed it with users. That round turned up frictions I would never have written into the script:

- Users apply for many jobs. They need a one-line reminder of which one this call is about.
- Candidates are anxious for explicit confirmation of the next step. The AI has to close every loop.
- Asking for the candidate's name first raises phishing suspicion. So I moved it later.

I picked Bland AI as the build platform. Bland gives you per-node prompts, loop conditions and live API integrations. Tuning an LLM for production is part engineering, part instrument-making.

Sidebar on the jargon. **Prompt engineering** gets you the first good output. **Context engineering** keeps the thousandth output good. The second one is what matters for a tool people put in front of paying candidates.

Each build cycle paired chat tests with live phone calls. Once the agent was reliable on my laptop, I stopped trusting my laptop. I built a fake job-application site with React, Tailwind and Supabase, hosted on Vercel. Submit the form, get a real call from Elliot, get a real calendar invite. Remote users could now test the entire experience.

Two later rounds caught the next two issues, both human before they were technical:

- The agent could be tricked into claiming it was a human. I added a global node that handles AI-identity questions with empathy, honesty and a little humour.
- Call-screening services on iOS and Android were breaking the opening. I mapped how those services work and split the greeting into two nodes so the screener can interleave a transcribed message in between.

While testing with recruiters in my network, I spoke to the Chief of Staff of a fast-growing company who could not hire as fast as they needed to. That conversation turned into a customer. Two collaborators came on board to take the product to market. A solo learning project is now a commercial concern.

Elliot lives at a public demo site with a battle-refined prompt, integrated tooling, and a database that hands warm candidates off to recruiters.

## Learnings

- **Build for the friction, not the demo.** The features that matter (AI-identity handling, call-screener compatibility) only showed up after live deployment. Roleplaying in a Zoom call could not have found them.
- **The candidate is the more sensitive user, not the recruiter.** Most AI recruitment tools optimise the recruiter side. Candidates can tell. Optimising for the candidate is the moat.
- **Customer development is the side door.** I was not selling Elliot. I was testing it. A user testing session became a sales conversation because the product was good and they had the pain.
