## Problem

NEOM's venture studio handed our team a 100-page market research report and a continent. Solve a healthcare problem in Africa. Make it commercially viable. Make it real.

Three Entrepreneurs-in-Residence. Four designers. Two subject-matter experts. Sixteen weeks.

The team was paralysed by the volume of data. So I made the first artifact: a visual map of healthcare opportunities against technological readiness. One thing fell out clearly. **Electronic health records were the gateway**. Without EHR rails you could not build anything more sophisticated downstream.

The Egypt-specific data added the second filter. **Non-communicable diseases were the urgent, under-addressed problem space**. Diabetes was the top of that list. Egypt had the population, the infrastructure maturity and the openness to innovation that the rest of the continent did not.

We had a venture challenge.

## Process

The team needed momentum, not more reading. I proposed the **Learning Lunge**: split into squads, two-day sprints, each squad chasing one slice of the problem space. Tech readiness, social impact, investment patterns, regulation. After the lunge, the team had a shared map, not a shared opinion.

The next problem was access. We were a remote English-speaking team trying to recruit Arabic-speaking healthcare professionals.

A premium recruitment firm quoted us prices that did not pass the smell test. I researched Egyptian doctor and nurse salaries, mapped local firms via business directories, shortlisted three with healthcare experience, and negotiated direct. **We signed a new partner at a quarter of the original quote. Net saving to the venture, roughly $50,000.** Days later the original vendor called back, suddenly desperate to compete.

The next blocker was language. Arabic-to-English translation lag was slowing our synthesis cadence by a day per cycle. So I designed a simultaneous-translation hack. A translator joined a second room on the call audio. She translated live into our team channel. My team could now transcribe, synthesise and push back-channel questions in real time. We were back to one-cycle-per-day.

We tested concepts as four-frame storyboards. Current context, the pain, the proposed solution, the improved situation. Three storyboards per user, randomised to reduce bias. After every fifth interview we sorted them into three buckets:

- **Kill** (no traction, no signal)
- **Modify** (tweak the framing, re-test)
- **Promote** (advance to the next stage)

Surviving value props went into a league table. They competed against each other across interview rounds. We ended with two prototypes worth building and one go-to-market story we could defend.

A research trip to Cairo turned one of our recruitment partners (a pharmacist who also ran a pharmacy chain) into our most valuable informant. I sat with him for an afternoon mapping patient journeys, information bottlenecks, cash flows and the informal workarounds that the official map ignores.

The week before the launch decision, Egypt's inflation rate jumped to 10%. The NEOM CFO killed the venture. Sixteen weeks of work, defunded.

I ran the final retrospective as a funeral service. I borrowed a priest's collar, used a church Zoom background, queued up liturgical music and wrote a sermon for the deceased project. The team laughed, cried a bit, and left with closure. One teammate later told me she rewatches the recording when she needs reminding that **good work is never wasted**.

## Learnings

- **Tooling for a team is a leadership artifact.** Yggdrasil was a Miro board. It was also the way the board presentation told itself. Build the artifact you want the room to remember.
- **Smell tests are an underused tool.** The $50,000 saving was not clever procurement. It was looking at one quote and asking why it felt high.
- **Closure is a deliverable.** The team did not get to ship the product. They did get to ship the work. The funeral was not a gimmick. It was the difference between "we failed" and "we made something the company chose not to take to market".
